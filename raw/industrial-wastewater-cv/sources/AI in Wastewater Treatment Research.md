# **Artificial Intelligence and Computer Vision in Industrial Wastewater Treatment: An Analysis of Coagulation Optimization and Oil Film Detection**

## **The Industrial Wastewater Context at Ansteel**

The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision into industrial wastewater treatment paradigms represents a critical evolution in environmental and chemical engineering, particularly within high-volume, complex manufacturing environments such as the steel industry. Facilities operating at the scale of the Ansteel Group in Anshan, China—which manages massive wastewater treatment systems like the Beidagou and Xidagou centers—process hundreds of thousands of tons of complex industrial effluent daily.1 The Xidagou Wastewater Treatment Center alone covers an area of 190,000 square meters and possesses a designed processing capacity of 220,000 tons.1 These facilities are tasked with achieving zero liquid discharge during non-rainy seasons and exceptionally high recycling rates, converting over 95% of industrial and domestic wastewater into grades suitable for reuse in production.1

The effluent from metallurgical processes, particularly hematite processing and beneficiation circuits, contains ultrafine particles (less than 10 ![][image1]) that settle slowly, form weak flocs, and lead to progressive accumulation of suspended solids.3 The highly alkaline nature of this slurry, often exhibiting a pH of approximately 12 due to calcium oxide addition during flotation, further complicates the physiochemical separation processes.3 To manage these complex matrices, treatment facilities rely heavily on chemical coagulation and flocculation, alongside the mechanical and biological separation of contaminants such as floating oil films derived from upstream riverine systems and internal plant operations.

Historically, determining the optimal dosage of coagulants (such as polyaluminum chloride) and flocculants (such as polyacrylamide) has relied on highly empirical, labor-intensive methodologies, primarily the manual jar test.4 These conventional methods suffer from substantial temporal delays—often requiring between two and four hours to yield actionable visual results regarding floc settling and supernatant turbidity.5 Consequently, static empirical models fail to capture the non-linear, dynamic fluctuations in real-time wastewater characteristics, leading to sub-optimal chemical dosing, excessive operational costs, and potential regulatory breaches.6

This analysis provides an exhaustive technical evaluation of two specific AI-driven solutions deployed to address these challenges using on-site data: the optimization of coagulant dosing through computer vision, feature engineering, and reinforcement learning; and the automated detection of floating oil films to preemptively trigger skimming and surfactant interventions, thereby preserving the efficacy of downstream biological aeration processes.

## **Formalizing the Coagulant Dosage Optimization Problem**

The prediction of optimal coagulant dosage in wastewater treatment is fundamentally a non-linear, multi-variable, large time-delay, and strong-coupling control problem.6 Water quality parameters such as raw water turbidity, pH, temperature, electrical conductivity, flow rate, and the concentration of dissolved organic matter interact in highly complex, synergistic ways that dictate the instantaneous chemical demand.6

Traditional predictive models approach this challenge as a static regression problem, utilizing current water quality parameters as inputs to output a deterministic dosage value.7 However, this formulation is fundamentally flawed when applied to dynamic, large-scale treatment plants due to inherent hydraulic retention times. The chemical processes of charge neutralization, double-layer compression, and the physical mechanism of sweep flocculation require substantial residence time—typically between two to four hours—to visibly manifest as fully formed, settleable flocs.5

Therefore, the problem is more accurately formalized as a Partially Observable Markov Decision Process (POMDP) where the state space consists of sequential multi-variate time-series data combined with real-time morphological images of floc formation. The objective is to map this sequential state space to an optimal continuous action space (the exact chemical dosage) in order to maximize a delayed reward (optimal effluent quality after the 4-hour retention time), while simultaneously minimizing chemical consumption and associated operational costs.10

### **Framing the Convolutional Neural Network: Sequence vs. Classification**

When applying Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to in-situ video data captured from the treatment plant's mixing or flocculation basins, a critical architectural decision must be made regarding whether to frame the network as a sequence model or a classification model.

Framing the CNN as a discrete classification network (e.g., classifying the visible floc states into rigid categories such as "under-dosed," "optimal," or "over-dosed") severely restricts the granularity of the control system. While a classification approach simplifies the immediate loss function during training, it fails to provide the continuous feedback required for precise pump modulation. Furthermore, the transition between an under-dosed and optimal state is continuous, not discrete, and a classification framework forces an artificial boundary that limits predictive precision.6

Instead, the CNN is optimally framed as a spatial feature extractor whose outputs (embeddings) are continuously fed into a sequential architecture. In this paradigm, the CNN processes individual frames from the video feed to capture the instantaneous spatial characteristics of the flocs. These morphological embeddings are then processed sequentially by architectures such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks or Dual-Stage Attention-Based Recurrent Neural Networks (DA-RNN).6

A DA-RNN framework, for instance, utilizes an attention-based encoder-decoder architecture to capture the non-linear and time-sequence relationships between chemical doses and source water quality changes.6 The first stage features an input attention mechanism that adaptively selects the most relevant input features at each time step, while the second stage utilizes temporal attention to select relevant encoder hidden states across all historical time steps.6 Therefore, the vision architecture serves as the spatial processing layer within a broader spatiotemporal sequence model, enabling the capture of both instantaneous floc morphology and the rate of change over the entire 2-to-4 hour chemical reaction window.

## **Morphological Preprocessing and Feature Extraction Pipeline**

To translate raw video data into actionable metrics for sequential dosage prediction, a robust data loading and morphological preprocessing pipeline is strictly required. Video data collected from major operational plants necessitates rigorous cleaning to remove ambient variations, surface reflections, and fluid turbulence before analytical feature engineering can commence.

The preprocessing pipeline typically initiates with the conversion of the raw RGB video frames into 8-bit greyscale images, reducing computational overhead while retaining structural fidelity.13 This is followed by noise filtration, frequently utilizing Gaussian or median filters to smooth the image and eliminate high-frequency artifacts caused by suspended micro-bubbles or sensor noise.15 To isolate individual flocs from the fluid background, adaptive thresholding techniques (such as Otsu's method) are employed, creating a binary mask where the regions of interest (the flocs) are distinctly separated from the continuous aqueous phase.16 Morphological opening and closing operations (erosion followed by dilation, and vice versa) are subsequently applied to the binary mask to remove isolated noise pixels and close small holes within the identified floc boundaries, ensuring contiguous object representation.17

Once the image is thoroughly preprocessed, advanced feature engineering is applied to extract highly specific data points that correlate mathematically with flocculation efficacy. Research into the automated image analysis of activated sludge and chemical flocs demonstrates that extracting a combination of first-order statistical features, second-order textural features, and geometric morphology is critical for accurate downstream modeling.18

### **Extraction of the 11 Major Floc Features**

The analysis of preprocessed floc images requires the extraction of specific quantitative descriptors. A comprehensive feature vector utilized in state-of-the-art vision models typically consists of 11 major features.18 These 11 features encapsulate the fundamental physics of the aggregating particles and are categorized into first-order statistics, second-order textures, and geometric parameters.

#### **First-Order Statistical Features**

First-order features describe the overall statistical distribution of pixel intensities within the region of interest, calculated directly from the original image histogram irrespective of the spatial arrangement of the pixels. These features provide insights into the density and light-scattering properties of the suspension.22

1. **Mean Intensity (![][image2]):** This represents the average brightness of the floc region. In bright-field illumination, denser, highly aggregated hematite flocs will present a distinctly different mean intensity compared to looser, highly hydrated organic aggregates.19  
2. **Intensity Variance (![][image3]):** This metric measures the spread or dispersion of pixel intensities around the mean, providing a baseline calculation for the internal heterogeneity of the floc composition.19 Higher variance indicates a mix of dense solid cores and translucent, porous boundary layers.  
3. **Skewness:** Skewness quantifies the asymmetry of the intensity distribution curve.18 A positive skew indicates a distribution with an asymmetric tail extending toward higher pixel values, helping to differentiate between solid, light-absorbing mineral flocs and highly reflective formations.  
4. **Kurtosis:** Kurtosis measures the "tailedness" of the pixel distribution, indicating the presence of extreme intensity variations.18 High kurtosis values suggest the presence of highly crystalline or metallic structures within the floc that create sharp peaks in pixel intensity due to specular reflection.

#### **Second-Order Textural Features**

Second-order features capture the spatial relationship between pairs of pixels, providing deep insight into the texture, roughness, and internal structural integrity of the floc. These are derived from a Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), which tabulates how often different combinations of pixel brightness values occur in a specific spatial relationship.19

5. **GLCM Contrast:** Contrast measures the local variations and spatial frequency present in an image. High contrast indicates a rough, highly irregular, and varied floc surface, which is a hallmark characteristic of rapid sweep-flocculation where particles agglomerate haphazardly.19 Mathematically, it is the sum of the squared differences between neighboring pixels.  
6. **GLCM Correlation:** This feature quantifies the linear dependency of grey levels on those of neighboring pixels. High correlation indicates the presence of structural directionality, such as the linearity found in filamentous bacteria formations or extended polymer bridging networks within the activated sludge.19  
7. **GLCM Energy (Angular Second Moment):** Energy provides a measure of textural uniformity. It represents the sum of squared elements in the GLCM. High energy signifies a highly uniform, tightly bound, and homogeneous floc structure, typical of well-consolidated charge-neutralized particles.19  
8. **GLCM Homogeneity:** Homogeneity measures the closeness of the distribution of elements in the GLCM to the GLCM diagonal. High homogeneity indicates smooth, well-consolidated flocs lacking abrupt internal variations in density.19

#### **Morphological and Geometric Features**

These features describe the physical shape, spatial footprint, and boundary characteristics of the segmented flocs, which are the primary determinants of settling velocity according to Stokes' Law.

9. **Equivalent Diameter:** This calculates the diameter of a circle that possesses the exact same area as the irregularly shaped floc.21 It provides a standardized, one-dimensional metric for floc size, which is critical for calculating theoretical settling rates in the clarifier.  
10. **Perimeter:** The total scalar length of the contiguous floc boundary.21 When evaluated in conjunction with the cross-sectional area, the perimeter helps determine the irregularity and surface roughness of the shape.  
11. **Fractal Dimension (![][image4]):** Fractal dimension is arguably the most critical morphological metric in modern coagulation physics.23 It quantifies the geometric complexity, self-similarity, and porosity of the floc. A higher fractal dimension (approaching 2.0 in a 2D projection) indicates a highly irregular, branching, and porous floc that retains significant internal water volume, resulting in slower settling. Conversely, a lower fractal dimension indicates a dense, tightly packed, spherical aggregate that will settle rapidly.25

By systematically engineering these 11 features, the raw, high-bandwidth video data is effectively compressed into a highly informative, low-dimensional mathematical vector that perfectly encapsulates the instantaneous physiochemical state of the coagulation process.

## **Object Detection Architectures: YOLO vs. Fast R-CNN**

To accurately extract the aforementioned 11 morphological features, the vision system must first precisely detect, localize, and segment individual flocs within the turbulent video frame. This is a complex object detection task, traditionally addressed by either single-stage grid-based detectors (such as the YOLO series) or two-stage region-proposal detectors (such as Fast R-CNN or Faster R-CNN).26 The selection between these architectures represents a critical trade-off between computational velocity and pixel-level precision.

### **Architecture of YOLO (You Only Look Once)**

The YOLO architecture is a single-stage detector that frames object detection as a unified, continuous regression problem, mapping straight from raw image pixels to bounding box coordinates and class probabilities within a single forward pass of the neural network.26 The network divides the input image into an ![][image5] grid, where each grid cell predicts a fixed number of bounding boxes and confidence scores for objects whose centers fall within that cell.28

The primary advantage of YOLO (particularly iterations such as YOLOv5, YOLOv8, or YOLOv9) is its exceptional inference speed, frequently exceeding 45 frames per second (FPS) on standard hardware.27 In the dynamic fluid environments of a wastewater treatment plant, this allows for the real-time, continuous tracking of floc growth and the immediate detection of process anomalies.28 However, because YOLO enforces spatial constraints through its grid system, it traditionally struggles with identifying highly clustered, severely overlapping, or exceptionally small particles (such as individual un-flocculated hematite fines) that occupy the same grid cell.29

### **Architecture of Fast R-CNN / Faster R-CNN**

In contrast, Fast R-CNN and Faster R-CNN are two-stage detectors. Faster R-CNN integrates a specialized Region Proposal Network (RPN) that generates highly probable regions of interest (anchors) across the image at various scales and aspect ratios.26 These proposed regions are then fed into a secondary convolutional classifier to refine the bounding box coordinates and assign class probabilities.26

While significantly more computationally intensive and slower than YOLO, the two-stage nature of Faster R-CNN—and its evolution, Mask R-CNN, which adds a pixel-level segmentation branch—yields superior precision and recall.27 When dealing with the dense, overlapping, and highly irregular shapes characteristic of activated sludge and complex chemical flocs, the region proposal mechanism is far more adept at delineating precise boundaries.27

Empirical evaluations within the domain of wastewater monitoring reveal distinct operational profiles for these architectures.

Studies comparing these algorithms for aquatic particle and sludge detection demonstrate that while Mask R-CNN can achieve pixel-level identification accuracies of 95.21%, newer YOLO iterations (such as YOLOv9) have narrowed the precision gap, achieving mean average precision (mAP@0.5) scores of up to 0.935 while maintaining inference times under 20 milliseconds.27 Furthermore, customized YOLO models incorporating Deformable Convolutional Networks (DCN) and Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) attention modules have shown 5-6% increases in precision for detecting irregularly shaped biological entities like rotifers and filamentous bacteria.30

Given the requirement to process continuous video streams for real-time dosage adjustment at Ansteel, highly optimized, lightweight YOLO architectures are generally preferred for their computational efficiency on edge devices, while Faster R-CNN remains the gold standard for highly granular, offline feature engineering where inference time is not the primary constraint.30

## **Dosage Prediction Regression: XGBoost vs. LightGBM**

Once the vision pipeline successfully extracts the 11 major morphological features, these metrics are concatenated with real-time scalar sensor data, encompassing variables such as raw water pH, temperature, influent turbidity, and hydraulic flow rate. This high-dimensional, multivariate vector must then be mapped to a precise, continuous output: the optimal coagulant dosage expressed in milligrams per liter (![][image6]).32

For this complex regression task, tree-based ensemble algorithms, specifically eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), represent the industry state-of-the-art, consistently outperforming Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Multiple Linear Regression (MLR) models in both accuracy and interpretability.33

### **Theoretical Framework of XGBoost**

XGBoost constructs an ensemble of decision trees sequentially, utilizing a gradient descent framework where each subsequent tree is specifically trained to minimize the residual errors (the loss gradient) of all prior trees.33 A defining characteristic of XGBoost is its level-wise (or depth-wise) tree growth strategy, wherein the algorithm expands the tree one level at a time across all nodes.

To prevent the model from overfitting to the highly correlated noise often present in wastewater sensor data, XGBoost incorporates advanced L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) regularization terms directly into its objective function.33 Furthermore, it handles sparse matrices and missing data exceptionally well—a critical feature in industrial environments where sensor packets are frequently dropped or disrupted by fouling.37 However, the level-wise growth and the need to pre-sort data to find optimal split points render XGBoost computationally intensive and slower to train on massive datasets.38

### **Theoretical Framework of LightGBM**

LightGBM was engineered specifically to address the computational bottlenecks of XGBoost when processing massive, high-dimensional datasets. Unlike XGBoost, LightGBM employs a leaf-wise (best-first) tree growth strategy.39 Instead of expanding all nodes at a given depth, it identifies the single leaf with the maximum delta loss and splits only that leaf. This strategy generally yields a lower overall loss and higher accuracy, though it is more prone to overfitting on very small datasets.39

To achieve extraordinary training speeds, LightGBM relies on two novel techniques: Gradient-based One-Side Sampling (GOSS) and Exclusive Feature Bundling (EFB).39 GOSS filters out data instances that have small gradients (those that are already well-predicted) and focuses the learning process strictly on instances with large gradients. EFB reduces dimensionality by bundling mutually exclusive features together without sacrificing information density.39 Furthermore, LightGBM utilizes histogram-based algorithms to bin continuous feature values into discrete buckets, drastically reducing the memory footprint and calculation time for optimal split points.33

Empirical evaluations comparing XGBoost and LightGBM in environmental engineering contexts demonstrate exceptional predictive capabilities for both algorithms, with subtle trade-offs depending on the specific operational constraints.33

In direct comparative studies for water quality and dosage prediction, XGBoost frequently achieves the highest absolute accuracy, with reported ![][image7] values reaching 0.992 and an exceptionally low Root Mean Square Error (RMSE) of 1.381.34 However, LightGBM performs nearly identically in accuracy while executing significantly faster, making it highly advantageous for continuous, automated retraining pipelines.40

Crucially, both models support SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis. SHAP values provide explicit, feature-level interpretability, unwrapping the "black box" of the ensemble models.33 SHAP interaction analyses reveal precisely how variables such as the 11 morphological features, Chemical Oxygen Demand (COD), and Biochemical Oxygen Demand (BOD) influence the final dosage prediction, allowing plant operators to trust the AI's logic and understand the non-linear dependencies driving the control system.33

## **Reinforcement Learning for Delayed Feedback Mitigation**

While supervised learning ensembles like XGBoost and LightGBM can predict exact dosages with high fidelity based on historical, steady-state data, they are fundamentally predictive rather than adaptive. They struggle to dynamically optimize the process when confronted with shifting environmental parameters, and critically, they are incapable of inherently optimizing for the 2-to-4 hour delay between chemical injection and observable flocculation out-turn.11

This retention delay—governed by the fixed hydraulic capacities of the mixing and sedimentation basins—creates a severe temporal credit assignment problem.10 If the effluent turbidity drops abruptly, standard supervised models cannot independently discern whether the success was caused by the dosage applied three hours prior, or a subtle change in jet mixing velocity two hours prior.

To resolve this temporal disconnect and transition the system from predictive forecasting to active control, Reinforcement Learning (RL) is integrated. RL algorithms are designed to learn optimal control policies through continuous, trial-and-error interaction with the environment, navigating the trade-off between exploration and exploitation to maximize a defined mathematical reward over time.8

### **Continuous Control Algorithms: DDPG and SAC**

Because the modulation of chemical dosing pumps and jet mixing velocities requires continuous, fine-grained adjustments rather than discrete steps, traditional tabular RL methods (like Q-Learning) are insufficient. Instead, advanced model-free, off-policy algorithms designed specifically for continuous action spaces are deployed, primarily the Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG) and the Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) algorithms.8

1. **Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG):** DDPG operates on an actor-critic architecture. The *actor network* ![][image8] observes the current state vector ![][image9] (comprising the 11 engineered image features, current turbidity, pH, and flow rate) and outputs a deterministic continuous action ![][image10] (e.g., adjust the coagulant dosing pump to deliver exactly 15.5 ![][image6]).8 Concurrently, the *critic network* ![][image11] evaluates the quality of this action by predicting the expected sum of discounted future rewards, guiding the actor toward optimal policies.8  
2. **Soft Actor-Critic (SAC):** SAC represents a significant evolution of the actor-critic framework by incorporating maximum entropy reinforcement learning.10 The reward function is augmented with an entropy term, forcing the agent to maximize a trade-off between the expected return and the entropy (randomness) of the policy. This encourages the agent to explore the state space widely, preventing it from converging prematurely on brittle, sub-optimal local minima—a frequent problem in highly stochastic wastewater environments.10

Furthermore, training an RL agent purely online in a live plant with a 4-hour iteration loop is practically impossible and highly unsafe. To overcome this, Deep Learning-based simulators—often powered by sequence models like LSTM networks—are constructed using historical plant data. These surrogate models simulate the delayed hydraulic feedback of the WWTP, predicting future states ![][image15] based on current actions and historical trajectories.10

The RL agent undergoes extensive offline reinforcement learning against this simulator. By training against the LSTM simulator with integrated delay distributions (constant or random delay models), the SAC agent learns a robust policy that natively anticipates the 4-hour retention time.10 Empirical deployments demonstrate that delay-aware SAC agents can achieve up to a 36% reduction in chemical emissions, a 77% reduction in target deviation, and a 20% to 25% overall improvement in flocculation efficiency, dynamically adjusting to incoming turbidity spikes long before human operators would detect a failure at the final clarifier effluent.10

## **Automated Identification of Floating Oil Films**

The second major AI vision solution deployed within the Ansteel facility addresses the critical upstream issue of oil contamination. Industrial effluents, particularly those sourced from massive metallurgical cooling processes, heavy machinery lubrication, or the localized riverine systems that run directly through the plant infrastructure, frequently contain substantial quantities of free, floating oil.46

If allowed to progress untreated through the wastewater collection network, these floating oil films present catastrophic risks to downstream secondary treatment systems, primarily by disrupting the biological aeration processes that are critical for activated sludge functionality.46

### **The Physics of Aeration Failure**

Secondary biological wastewater treatment relies absolutely on maintaining strict dissolved oxygen (DO) concentrations (typically between 1 and 3 ![][image6]) in aeration basins.46 This oxygen supports the aerobic metabolism of the microbial consortium responsible for the degradation of organic carbon and nitrification processes.46 Oxygen is typically supplied via pressurized fine-bubble diffusers located at the bottom of the basin.49

The efficiency of this critical oxygen transfer from the gaseous bubble phase into the liquid aqueous phase is mathematically governed by the volumetric mass transfer coefficient, denoted as ![][image16], where ![][image17] is the liquid-side mass transfer coefficient and ![][image10] is the specific interfacial area. According to the two-film theory of mass transfer, when an upstream oil slick enters the aeration basin, the hydrophobic hydrocarbons rapidly accumulate at all available gas-liquid interfaces.

This accumulation creates a highly resistant physical boundary layer around the rising air bubbles and across the macroscopic surface of the basin.50 The presence of surfactants, complex greases, and contiguous oil films drastically increases the resistance to mass transfer, thereby severely depressing the ![][image17] value. Empirical studies demonstrate that oil contamination can suppress the overall ![][image16] value significantly, physically starving the aerobic bacteria of oxygen despite continuous compressor operation.50

The resulting hypoxia induces rapid microbial die-off, forces the basin into an unwanted septic or strict denitrification state, and causes systemic process failure characterized by excessive foaming, toxicity spikes, and the discharge of non-compliant, highly polluted effluent.46

### **Computer Vision for Oil Slick Detection**

To prevent such catastrophic aeration failure, an automated early-warning system utilizing computer vision is essential to detect the presence of oil films on the surface of the upstream river or influent channels before the water reaches the biological reactors.

The optical detection of oil spills in highly variable, outdoor aquatic environments is exceptionally challenging due to changing ambient lighting conditions, sun glint, and chaotic water turbulence.53 To overcome these optical variations, multi-modal sensor arrays are utilized.

Standard RGB optical cameras, employing edge detection algorithms (such as Canny edge detection) or YOLO-based bounding systems, are effective during daylight hours for identifying the distinct chromatic signatures, diffraction patterns, and sheen of thin oil films.17 However, to ensure unbroken 24-hour operation, thermal infrared (IR) cameras are heavily deployed.54 Oil films possess intrinsically different thermal emissivities and specific heat capacities compared to the surrounding water matrix. This allows IR sensors to capture a distinct thermal contrast regardless of ambient light, rendering the oil slicks highly visible even in complete darkness.54

The automated identification process relies heavily on deep learning segmentation models. Unlike the flocculation application where bounding boxes are sufficient, detecting oil slicks requires semantic or instance segmentation to determine the exact boundary, total surface area coverage, and estimated volumetric thickness of the amorphous spill.55 Algorithms such as Mask R-CNN or adapted YOLO-seg architectures process the multi-modal camera feeds, predicting the spatial extent of the film and continuously calculating the percentage of the water surface area contaminated.17 When this calculated contamination exceeds a pre-defined critical threshold (e.g., 0.5% coverage), the AI automatically triggers physical and chemical intervention protocols.17

## **Automated Interventions: Skimming and Surfactant Dosing**

Upon positive identification and volumetric estimation of a threatening oil film, the AI control system orchestrates a rapid, two-pronged remediation strategy before the effluent reaches the vulnerable aeration basins: mechanical skimming and surfactant injection.46

### **Mechanical Skimming Dynamics**

For thick, pooled oil or macroscopic free-floating layers (often exceeding 1 cm in thickness), physical mechanical removal is the most efficient primary intervention.57 The AI system automatically modulates the operation of API gravity separators, Dissolved Air Flotation (DAF) units, or active surface skimmers deployed in the upstream channels.46

By dynamically altering baffle flow patterns and engaging rotary flight scrapers or deploying specialized inflatable containment booms, the concentrated free oil is mechanically corralled and dragged into collection hoppers.58 This recovered waste oil is subsequently transferred via pumps to temporary storage tanks for eventual incineration or secondary phase-separation recovery.59

### **Surfactant Injection and Emulsification Chemistry**

While mechanical skimming is highly effective for bulk oil, it is often physically incapable of capturing very thin films, microscopic sheens, or partially emulsified droplets that have already begun to disperse in the turbulent flow.57 To address this residual contamination, the AI triggers the precise, volumetrically calculated dosing of chemical surfactants.63

Surfactants are amphiphilic molecules, possessing both a hydrophilic (water-attracting) head and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) tail. When dosed into the upstream flow, these molecules rapidly migrate to and orient themselves along the oil-water interface. This initiates two primary mechanisms of action that neutralize the threat to downstream aeration:

1. **Reduction of Interfacial Tension:** The presence of the surfactant molecules sharply decreases the thermodynamic interfacial tension between the water and the contiguous oil film. This sudden drop in surface tension alters the contact angle, forcing the continuous film to "roll up" and spontaneously break apart into microscopic, discrete oil droplets.64  
2. **Micelle Formation and Stabilization:** Once broken into discrete droplets, the surfactant molecules completely encapsulate the oil, forming stable structures known as micelles. The hydrophilic heads face outward into the aqueous phase, providing steric and electrostatic repulsion between the individual micelles. This repulsion prevents the oil droplets from undergoing Ostwald ripening or re-coalescing back into a continuous film.57 Crucially, it also prevents the hydrophobic oil from adhering to the structural walls of the aeration tanks or coating the surface of the rising oxygen bubbles.57

By chemically fracturing the contiguous film into a stable, highly dispersed micro-emulsion, the immediate physical boundary layer restricting oxygen mass transfer (![][image16]) is eliminated. The resulting highly dispersed hydrocarbons can then be subjected to targeted biodegradation by the established aerobic microbial consortia within the activated sludge, completely safeguarding the biological integrity of the primary aeration basins.46

## **Conclusion**

The deployment of advanced artificial intelligence and computer vision solutions within the Ansteel wastewater treatment infrastructure marks a fundamental paradigm shift from reactive, heuristic-based plant operations to proactive, deterministic, data-driven optimization.

In the complex domain of coagulant dosage prediction, the fusion of Convolutional Neural Networks for real-time morphological feature extraction with advanced gradient boosting algorithms (XGBoost and LightGBM) and deep reinforcement learning (DDPG and SAC) successfully resolves the persistent challenges of non-linear chemical dynamics and extensive hydraulic time delays. By abstracting raw video data into mathematically robust first- and second-order textural features, the predictive models ensure highly precise chemical dosing, significantly reducing operational waste and ensuring strict regulatory compliance.

Concurrently, the application of multi-modal computer vision to identify and segment upstream oil films serves as an indispensable, automated fail-safe for the facility’s biological reactors. By dynamically monitoring surface contamination via thermal and optical sensors, the AI seamlessly orchestrates physical mechanical skimming and precise chemical surfactant interventions. This proactive remediation breaks down contiguous hydrocarbon layers, preempting the catastrophic degradation of oxygen mass transfer coefficients and preserving the absolute stability of the aerobic digestion process. Collectively, these implemented technologies underscore the profound operational, economic, and environmental benefits of integrating sophisticated, deterministic AI architectures into complex, large-scale industrial ecologies.
