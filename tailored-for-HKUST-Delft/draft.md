Thought for a couple of seconds

A research statement / statement of research achievement and a research proposal are related, but they answer different questions.

The simplest distinction is:

Research statement / achievement:
“What have I already done as a researcher, and what does it show about my potential?”

Research proposal:
“What do I want to investigate next, why does it matter, and why is this lab/program a good place to do it?”

1. Research statement / research achievement

This is mostly about your past and present research experience.

It should not simply be a list of projects. The goal is to show that you understand research: how to ask questions, choose methods, interpret results, handle uncertainty, and learn from problems.

A strong research achievement statement usually includes:

Your research background

Briefly explain the area you have worked in. For example:

My previous research focused on machine learning methods for medical image segmentation, especially improving model robustness under limited labeled data.

This gives the reader context.

Your main projects

For each important project, explain:

What problem you studied
Why the problem mattered
What your specific role was
What methods or tools you used
What you found or produced
What you learned from it

The most important part is your contribution. Admissions committees want to know what you did, not just what the group did.

For example, instead of writing:

I worked on a project about battery materials.

Write something like:

I investigated how electrolyte composition affected cycling stability in lithium-metal batteries. I prepared samples, ran electrochemical tests, analyzed capacity-retention data, and helped identify a concentration range that reduced early-stage degradation.

Your achievements

Mention concrete outcomes where relevant:

Publications
Manuscripts in preparation
Conference presentations
Posters
Awards
Datasets
Code
Experimental results
Technical skills gained
Independent research decisions you made

But do not exaggerate. A small but clearly explained contribution is better than a vague big claim.

Your development as a researcher

This is where you can discuss what the experience taught you. For example:

This project taught me that model performance alone is not enough; I became interested in interpretability because the best-performing model was not necessarily the most clinically useful one.

This helps bridge into your future interests.

2. Research proposal

The proposal is about your future research direction.

For a PhD application, it usually does not need to be a perfect, fixed plan. PhD research changes. But it should show that you can think like a researcher and that your interests fit the program.

A strong research proposal usually includes:

A clear research area

Start with the broad area you want to work in.

For example:

I am interested in developing data-efficient machine learning methods for biological imaging, particularly methods that can generalize across labs, devices, and patient populations.

This is better than saying only:

I am interested in AI and healthcare.

The research problem or gap

Explain what is not yet solved.

For example:

Although deep learning models have achieved strong performance on benchmark datasets, many fail when applied to data from new hospitals or imaging devices. This limits their clinical reliability.

This shows you understand the field, not just your personal interest.

Possible research questions

You can include 2–3 questions such as:

How can models adapt to new imaging domains with minimal labeled data?
Can uncertainty estimation help identify cases where a model should defer to human experts?
How can robustness be evaluated beyond standard accuracy metrics?

These do not have to be final dissertation questions. They show direction.

Possible methods

Explain how you might study the problem:

I would explore domain adaptation, self-supervised learning, and uncertainty-aware evaluation. Depending on data availability, I would test these methods on multi-institutional imaging datasets.

The methods should be plausible, not overly detailed.

Why this lab/program fits

Yes, you should tailor this part to the lab’s recent work.

But do it carefully. Do not just flatter the professor or summarize their papers. Instead, connect your interests to their work.

Weak version:

Professor X’s recent publications are very impressive, and I want to join the lab.

Stronger version:

Professor X’s recent work on self-supervised representation learning for medical imaging aligns closely with my interest in reducing annotation dependence. I am especially interested in extending these ideas toward cross-domain robustness and uncertainty-aware deployment.

This shows fit and intellectual engagement.

Is it about your interests, your learning path, or the lab’s publications?

It is a combination, but the balance is different for each document.

For the research achievement statement, emphasize:

past work, concrete contributions, skills, results, and growth.

For the research proposal, emphasize:

future research questions, motivation, possible methods, and fit with the lab.

Your interests matter, but they should be presented as research interests, not only personal preferences. Instead of saying:

I am happy to work on computer vision.

Say:

I am interested in computer vision problems where model reliability under distribution shift is a central challenge.

Your expected learning path can be mentioned, but it should not dominate. A PhD application is not mainly asking, “What classes do you want to take?” It is asking, “Can this person become an independent researcher?”

So instead of focusing on:

I want to learn X, Y, and Z.

Frame it as:

To pursue these questions, I hope to deepen my training in X, Y, and Z.

And yes, you should tailor the proposal to the lab’s recent publications, but not so narrowly that you sound like you only want to copy their current work. A good proposal says:

I understand what your lab does, and I can imagine a natural next direction that connects my background with your research.

A practical structure

For a research achievement statement, you can use this structure:

Brief overview of your research area
Project 1: problem, role, methods, outcome
Project 2: problem, role, methods, outcome
Skills and research maturity gained
How this background motivates your PhD direction

For a research proposal, you can use this structure:

Broad research interest
Specific problem/gap
Research questions
Possible methods/data/theoretical approach
Fit with the professor/lab/program
Long-term goal
The key mindset

Do not write it as:

“Here is what I would be happy to do.”

Write it more like:

“Here is the research direction I am prepared to pursue, why it matters, how my background supports it, and why this lab is a strong intellectual fit.”

You do not need to sound like you already know your entire PhD dissertation. You do need to sound thoughtful, prepared, and specific enough that a professor can imagine working with you.

Recent VLA models such as π0.5/π0.7 and world/action models such as DreamZero and DreamDojo show promising routes toward generalist robot learning, but they also highlight that architecture and data recipes must co-evolve. I am therefore interested in human-in-the-loop data flywheels for embodied world models, where humans can collect dexterous demonstrations, intervene during failed rollouts, and provide corrective signals that improve model robustness.

Part 2: HCI / BCI motivation

My interest in human-centered interaction was strengthened by observing a disabled developer demonstrate a brain-signal-controlled wheelchair. The system has recently progressed from forward/backward commands to multi-directional movement control, making the practical value of human-machine control very tangible. At the same time, it reveals open research questions in low-SNR brain-signal decoding, cognitive load, latency, safety, and shared autonomy. This motivates me to study how AI systems can infer human intention from weak, noisy, or partial signals and translate that intention into reliable embodied action.

Part 3: Fusion

I hope to explore embodied AI systems that combine physical-world models with human-in-the-loop interaction. One possible direction is to use world/action models to predict the physical consequences of robot actions, while using human feedback, intervention, or intention signals to guide data collection, correct failures, and improve policy behavior. Such a system would connect embodied AI, HCI, BCI, and real-time multimodal interaction, which aligns with the program’s focus on Human-AI Symbiotic Intelligence and Human-AI Co-Creativity.
